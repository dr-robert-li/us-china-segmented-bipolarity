## 4. The arrest of the Gang of Four: date, mechanics, executing unit, ordering authority

### The primary scholarly account

Frederick C. Teiwes (**Emeritus Professor of Chinese Politics, University of Sydney**) and Warren Sun (**retired Reader in Chinese Studies, Monash University**) — affiliations confirmed at [University of Sydney China Studies Centre working paper](https://www.sydney.edu.au/content/dam/corporate/documents/china-studies-centre/working-paper/fred_teiwes_feb_2022.pdf) — in ["China's October Coup That Ended the Maoist Era"](https://www.bannedthought.net/China/MaoEra/GPCR/BourgeoisCommentary/China'sOctoberCoupThatEndedTheMaoistEra-Sun-Teiwes.pdf):

- **The ruse:** the plan was "to announce on the morning of the 6th a Politburo Standing Committee meeting for that evening to examine the final proof of Volume 5 of Mao's *Selected Works*" (p.360).
- **The arrests:** "Wang, Zhang and then Yao were lured to the meeting where the arrest was organized by Wang Dongxing, and the charges against them read by Hua"; "Shortly afterward Mao Yuanxin and finally Jiang Qing were arrested separately in their residences"; "The decisive action had taken no more than 35 minutes" (p.360).
- **Timing:** "With the arrests effected about 8 p.m., an emergency Politburo meeting was convened at 10 pm at Ye's residence at Mount Yuquan" (p.360).
- **Who ordered it:** "only Hua was in the position to issue orders…"; "Our verdict … is that primary credit must go to Hua Guofeng" (p.362). Ye Jianying and Wang Dongxing are treated as co-principals, with Wang Dongxing organising the physical arrest.

### Was it at a Politburo meeting convened for the purpose?

**Partly — and this is the key nuance.** The meeting to which Wang Hongwen, Zhang Chunqiao and Yao Wenyuan were summoned was a **Politburo Standing Committee** meeting announced under a false pretext (proofreading Mao's *Selected Works*), i.e. a decoy, not a deliberative body convened to decide the arrests ([Teiwes & Sun, p.360](https://www.bannedthought.net/China/MaoEra/GPCR/BourgeoisCommentary/China'sOctoberCoupThatEndedTheMaoistEra-Sun-Teiwes.pdf)). Jiang Qing and Mao Yuanxin were seized separately at their residences, not at any meeting ([same, p.360](https://www.bannedthought.net/China/MaoEra/GPCR/BourgeoisCommentary/China'sOctoberCoupThatEndedTheMaoistEra-Sun-Teiwes.pdf)). The **real** Politburo meeting convened at 10 p.m., *after* the fact ([same, p.360](https://www.bannedthought.net/China/MaoEra/GPCR/BourgeoisCommentary/China'sOctoberCoupThatEndedTheMaoistEra-Sun-Teiwes.pdf)).

### Which unit physically carried out the arrests — Central Guard Regiment / Unit 8341?

**Not confirmed, and only partially supported.** Teiwes and Sun state the arrest was "organized by Wang Dongxing" but the fetched pages do **not** name the executing unit ([Teiwes & Sun, p.360](https://www.bannedthought.net/China/MaoEra/GPCR/BourgeoisCommentary/China'sOctoberCoupThatEndedTheMaoistEra-Sun-Teiwes.pdf)). Immanuel C. Y. Hsü states that Wang Dongxing commanded "the 20,000-man 8341 special unit" and that in the operation "Ye [was] the mastermind, Hua laying out the plan of action, and Wang implementing it" ([Hsü, *China Without Mao*, 2nd edn, Oxford University Press, ch. "The Smashing of the Gang of Four", published online 10 May 1990](https://academic.oup.com/book/25557/chapter/192860516?searchresult=1)). That is an inference-supporting chain — Wang Dongxing commanded 8341, and Wang Dongxing implemented the arrests — but **no fetched source states in terms that Unit 8341 / the Central Guard Regiment physically made the arrests**. Recorded as **partially corroborated, not confirmed**. See Gaps §4.

### Date

Both scholarly accounts place the operation on the night of **6 October 1976** (Teiwes & Sun) or **midnight of 5 October 1976** (Hsü) — see the conflict recorded below and in Gaps §5.

## 5. Constitutionality: did the arrest and the transfer to Hua follow the 1973 CCP Constitution?

### What the 1973 Constitution actually provided

Full text fetched at [marxists.org, Constitution of the Communist Party of China (1973)](https://www.marxists.org/subject/china/documents/cpc/CONSTITUTION_CPC.htm); the same text appears in the [1973 China Reconstructs supplement PDF](https://www.marxists.org/subject/china/china-reconstructs/1973/CR1973-11-Sup.pdf). It runs to **twelve articles**.

- **Article 9 (verbatim):** "The plenary session of the Central Committee of the Party elects the Political Bureau of the Central Committee, the Standing Committee of the Political Bureau of the Central Committee and the Chairman and Vice-Chairmen of the Central Committee. … When the Central Committee is not in plenary session, the Political Bureau of the Central Committee and its Standing Committee exercise the functions and powers of the Central Committee." ([1973 CCP Constitution](https://www.marxists.org/subject/china/documents/cpc/CONSTITUTION_CPC.htm))
- **Article 4 (verbatim, disciplinary measures):** the available measures are "warning, serious warning, removal from posts in the Party, placing on probation within the Party, or expulsion from the Party" ([1973 CCP Constitution](https://www.marxists.org/subject/china/documents/cpc/CONSTITUTION_CPC.htm)). This is a general provision applicable to all Party members.
- **Article 8:** the National Party Congress meets every five years ([1973 CCP Constitution](https://www.marxists.org/subject/china/documents/cpc/CONSTITUTION_CPC.htm)).

**Findings on the two specific questions:**

1. **Was there a procedure for removing Politburo members?** **No specific procedure.** The 1973 text contains no article governing the removal of a Politburo member as such, no quorum or majority requirement, and no equivalent of the later two-thirds-of-a-plenum rule. The only removal-adjacent language is the generic Article 4 disciplinary list ([1973 CCP Constitution](https://www.marxists.org/subject/china/documents/cpc/CONSTITUTION_CPC.htm)). *(Contrast with the 2007/2012 Constitutions, which do have an explicit rule — see Episode 2 §9.)*
2. **Was there a succession procedure for the Chairman?** **No.** Article 9 provides only for election of the Chairman by a plenary session of the Central Committee; there is no vacancy, acting-Chairman, or automatic-succession provision ([1973 CCP Constitution](https://www.marxists.org/subject/china/documents/cpc/CONSTITUTION_CPC.htm)).

### Was the arrest authorised by any Party organ beforehand?

**No.** Teiwes and Sun: "the crucial act was taken without reference to the Politburo as a whole, which was only subsequently informed of the fait accompli" (p.353), and "This, of course, did not mean that formal norms were rigorously adhered to, with the Politburo as such ignorant of the critical and **inherently illegal** decision to act against the 'gang'" (p.353) ([Teiwes & Sun](https://www.bannedthought.net/China/MaoEra/GPCR/BourgeoisCommentary/China'sOctoberCoupThatEndedTheMaoistEra-Sun-Teiwes.pdf)). They also record that the lawful alternative was considered and rejected: "The only viable options actually considered seem to have been calling a Central Committee meeting to remove the radicals legally, or to arrest them" (p.364), and note "Party organizational principles, principles which argued against what happened on October 6 and led the old revolutionaries to consider legal methods…" (p.351) ([same](https://www.bannedthought.net/China/MaoEra/GPCR/BourgeoisCommentary/China'sOctoberCoupThatEndedTheMaoistEra-Sun-Teiwes.pdf)).

### When was it ratified, and by what body?

Two-stage ratification:

1. **Politburo, immediately after the fact.** An emergency Politburo meeting convened at 10 p.m. the same night at Ye Jianying's residence at Mount Yuquan ([Teiwes & Sun, p.360](https://www.bannedthought.net/China/MaoEra/GPCR/BourgeoisCommentary/China'sOctoberCoupThatEndedTheMaoistEra-Sun-Teiwes.pdf)). Hsü's account: "On October 7 Hua Guofeng and Wang Dongxing each delivered two reports and Ye Jianying, one, to the Politburo… The grateful Politburo named Hua chairman of the party Central Committee and concurrently chairman of the Military Commission" ([Hsü, OUP](https://academic.oup.com/book/25557/chapter/192860516?searchresult=1)).
2. **Central Committee, nine months later.** The **3rd Plenary Session of the 10th Central Committee, 16–21 July 1977**, "permanently expelled Wang Hongwen, Zhang Chunqiao, Jiang Qing, and Yao Wenyuan from the Party and revoked all their Party and non-Party positions", and adopted the "Resolution to Confirm Comrade Hua Guofeng's Positions as Chairman of the Central Committee of the Communist Party of China and Chairman of the Central Military Commission of the Communist Party of China" ([gov.cn](https://www.gov.cn/test/2007-08/28/content_729620.htm)).

### Scholarly characterisations — both sides

**(a) Extra-legal / coup — Teiwes (University of Sydney) and Sun (Monash University).** The paper is titled "China's October Coup That Ended the Maoist Era". Key formulations: the decision to act against the gang was "**inherently illegal**" (p.353); it "was taken without reference to the Politburo as a whole, which was only subsequently informed of the fait accompli" (p.353); it contravened "Party organizational principles, principles which argued against what happened on October 6" (p.351) ([Teiwes & Sun](https://www.bannedthought.net/China/MaoEra/GPCR/BourgeoisCommentary/China'sOctoberCoupThatEndedTheMaoistEra-Sun-Teiwes.pdf)).

**(b) "Dubious legality" but accepted — Immanuel C. Y. Hsü, *China Without Mao* (Oxford University Press).** "Strictly speaking, the process of the selection of Hua as Mao's successor was of dubious legality. Article 9 of the party constitution stipulated that the party chairman must be elected in a plenary session of the Central Committee, and the Third Plenary Session of the Tenth Central Committee had not yet met. But the party and the country were willing to overlook the legal formality and make Hua the new leader at once." Hsü also describes the military's "key role … in the 'palace revolution'" ([Hsü, OUP](https://academic.oup.com/book/25557/chapter/192860516?searchresult=1)).

**(c) Usurpation by the winners, with the losers acting lawfully — Chien Liu, Department of Sociology, Wagner College, in *Modern China Studies* (2016, No. 2).** "I show that it is the accuser, the Hua faction, who usurped the power by military force; in contrast, the accused, the Jiang faction attempted to seize the political power through legitimate procedures." ([Chien Liu, *Modern China Studies*](https://www.modernchinastudies.org/us/issues/past-issues/123-mcs-2016-issue-2/1410-smashing-the-gang-of-four-revisited-it-is-hua-goufeng-who-usurped-the-power-but-not-the-gang-of-four-chien-liu-department-of-sociology-wagner-college.html))

**(d) Nearest thing found to a "lawful" reading — and it is not a claim of legality.** Teiwes and Sun note that the surviving elite treated Hua's authority as legitimate after the fact: "they quite gratefully accepted the actions and legitimacy of the new formal leader, Hua Guofeng" (p.363) ([Teiwes & Sun](https://www.bannedthought.net/China/MaoEra/GPCR/BourgeoisCommentary/China'sOctoberCoupThatEndedTheMaoistEra-Sun-Teiwes.pdf)). **No fetched source argues that the October 1976 arrests were constitutionally lawful.** The brief asked for quotations on both sides "if both exist"; on the fetched evidence, a scholarly defence of the arrests' *legality* does not exist. What exists is a spectrum from "inherently illegal" (Teiwes & Sun) through "dubious legality, overlooked" (Hsü) to "usurpation by military force" (Chien Liu). Recorded in Gaps §6.

## 6. Was Hua's assumption of the Party Chairmanship confirmed by the Central Committee, and when?

**Yes — at the 3rd Plenary Session of the 10th Central Committee, 16–21 July 1977**, which "Adopted the Resolution to Confirm Comrade Hua Guofeng's Positions as Chairman of the Central Committee of the Communist Party of China and Chairman of the Central Military Commission of the Communist Party of China" ([gov.cn record of the 10th CC's plenary sessions](https://www.gov.cn/test/2007-08/28/content_729620.htm)).

Interval between assumption of the post (7 October 1976, by Politburo action per [Hsü](https://academic.oup.com/book/25557/chapter/192860516?searchresult=1)) and Central Committee confirmation: **approximately nine months**. During that interval Hua held the Chairmanship without the plenary-session election that Article 9 required ([1973 CCP Constitution](https://www.marxists.org/subject/china/documents/cpc/CONSTITUTION_CPC.htm)) — exactly the defect Hsü identifies.

## 7. Military movements around Beijing, October 1976

### Command constraint

"any unit larger than a company (*lian*) could not be moved without the permission of the MAC—and Chen Xilian … had authority over that body" ([Teiwes & Sun, p.356](https://www.bannedthought.net/China/MaoEra/GPCR/BourgeoisCommentary/China'sOctoberCoupThatEndedTheMaoistEra-Sun-Teiwes.pdf)).

### Beijing Garrison Command — units tasked to secure key installations

"Hua told Wu the Beijing Garrison would have to secure the Xinhua News Agency, the People's Radio Station, the People's Daily, the airport and other important units, and that he (Wu) would take charge of the Garrison Command, an order effected by Chen Xilian using his authority in the MAC" ([Teiwes & Sun, p.359](https://www.bannedthought.net/China/MaoEra/GPCR/BourgeoisCommentary/China'sOctoberCoupThatEndedTheMaoistEra-Sun-Teiwes.pdf)). Garrison Commander Wu Zhong was assessed as reliable by the plotters ([same, p.359](https://www.bannedthought.net/China/MaoEra/GPCR/BourgeoisCommentary/China'sOctoberCoupThatEndedTheMaoistEra-Sun-Teiwes.pdf)). This is an affirmative finding: **armed units under the Beijing Garrison Command were used to secure media and transport nodes in the capital as part of the operation.**

### Tank units near Beijing

"The case of the tank units near Beijing has more substance"; "Hua told him on October 3 or 4 that Ye was worried about a tank division at Changping in the suburbs of Beijing", the source of the worry being Zhang Chunqiao's brother Zhang Qiuqiao in the PLA General Political Department ([Teiwes & Sun, p.358](https://www.bannedthought.net/China/MaoEra/GPCR/BourgeoisCommentary/China'sOctoberCoupThatEndedTheMaoistEra-Sun-Teiwes.pdf)).

### Mao Yuanxin's alleged Shenyang mobilisation — REFUTED

The allegation: "an alleged October 2 order from Mao Yuanxin, now back at his post in the Shenyang Military Region, to send an armored division under … Sun Yuguo, to the capital, and maneuvers of tank units near Beijing" ([Teiwes & Sun, p.358](https://www.bannedthought.net/China/MaoEra/GPCR/BourgeoisCommentary/China'sOctoberCoupThatEndedTheMaoistEra-Sun-Teiwes.pdf)).

The refutation, in the same source: "**The threat from the Shenyang troops is most easily dismissed as mere rumor.**" "According to a Party historian who questioned Shenyang Military Region Commander Li Desheng, Li denied that any such thing happened." "Subsequently, however, Sun Yuguo was relieved of his command, although not expelled from the Party." ([Teiwes & Sun, p.358](https://www.bannedthought.net/China/MaoEra/GPCR/BourgeoisCommentary/China'sOctoberCoupThatEndedTheMaoistEra-Sun-Teiwes.pdf))

**Adjudication: the Mao Yuanxin Shenyang mobilisation claim is refuted by the best specialist source available**, with the caveat that the refutation rests on a denial by the implicated regional commander relayed through an unnamed Party historian, plus the unexplained subsequent removal of Sun Yuguo — which the authors themselves do not explain away.

### The "38th Army"

**n.a. from the strongest source.** Teiwes and Sun's account contains **no reference to the 38th Army**. Hsü gives a materially different and more dramatic account: a secret meeting "in the early morning of October 5" attended by Hua, Ye, Wang Dongxing, "Beijing Garrison Commander Chen Xilian" and Li Xiannian, after which "Hua dispatched soldiers to guard the Great Wall against possible attack from Mukden and transferred another regiment to Beijing to watch over the 38th regiment. Meanwhile, the Guangzhou Military Region was alerted to ready two divisions for airlift to Beijing on instant notice." ([Hsü, OUP](https://academic.oup.com/book/25557/chapter/192860516?searchresult=1)) Note Hsü says "38th **regiment**", not 38th Army. See Gaps §5 and §7.

### Commanders arrested

- Mao Yuanxin: arrested during the operation ([Teiwes & Sun, p.360](https://www.bannedthought.net/China/MaoEra/GPCR/BourgeoisCommentary/China'sOctoberCoupThatEndedTheMaoistEra-Sun-Teiwes.pdf)).

# EPISODE 2 — The 2012–13 transition (negative control)

## 8. Full members of the Politburo elected by the 17th Central Committee (October 2007)

**25 full members.**

- Congressional Research Service: "The 17th Party Congress announced the selection of a **25-member Politburo** (including 9 new members — 10 if counting as 'new' the promotion of a previous alternate to a full member)"; the Congress met **15–21 October 2007** ([CRS Report RS22767, Kerry Dumbaugh, 5 December 2007, PDF](https://sgp.fas.org/crs/row/RS22767.pdf)).
- Independent confirmation from a specialist institution: Alice Miller (Hoover Institution), *China Leadership Monitor* No. 40, gives the 17th Central Committee Politburo as **25** members, and refers to "the 24 members of the outgoing 17th Central Committee Politburo who remained after the removal of Bo Xilai earlier in 2012" ([Alice Miller, "The New Party Politburo Leadership", *China Leadership Monitor* 40, PDF](https://www.hoover.org/sites/default/files/research/docs/CLM40AM.pdf)).
- Enumerated official name list of the 25, elected 22 October 2007 at the 1st Plenary Session of the 17th CC ([china.org.cn](http://www.china.org.cn/english/congress/229262.htm)).

**Alternates:** the official list page does not mention any Politburo alternate for the 17th CC ([china.org.cn](http://www.china.org.cn/english/congress/229262.htm)); CRS notes that the 16th Politburo had 24 members plus 1 alternate ([CRS RS22767](https://sgp.fas.org/crs/row/RS22767.pdf)), implying the practice lapsed. **A positive statement that the 17th Politburo had zero alternates was not found — recorded as n.a.** See Gaps §8.

## 9. Politburo members of that body removed for disciplinary or political reasons, ~2011–2013

**One: Bo Xilai.** Alice Miller confirms he was the only such removal — "the 24 members of the outgoing 17th Central Committee Politburo who remained after the removal of Bo Xilai earlier in 2012" ([*China Leadership Monitor* 40](https://www.hoover.org/sites/default/files/research/docs/CLM40AM.pdf)).

### Sequence, from the official Xinhua account

| Step | Date | Action | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Suspension | **10 April 2012** | "the CPC Central Committee decided to suspend Bo's membership in the CPC Central Committee Political Bureau and the CPC Central Committee and the CCDI filed the case for investigation" | [Xinhua via China Daily, 28 September 2012](https://www.chinadaily.com.cn/china/2012-09/28/content_15791527.htm) |
| Politburo decision to expel | **Friday 28 September 2012** | Politburo meeting expelled Bo from the Party and removed him from public office, on a CCDI investigation report | [China Daily / Xinhua](https://www.chinadaily.com.cn/china/2012-09/28/content_15791527.htm) |
| Central Committee endorsement | **last day of the four-day 7th plenum of the 17th CC** (the plenum "wrapped up … yesterday" in a report dated 5 November 2012, i.e. **4 November 2012**) | "The plenum also endorsed an earlier decision by the Politburo to expel Bo, the former Chongqing party boss, as well as Liu Zhijun, the former railway minister, Xinhua said." Two alternates were promoted to full CC membership "filling the void left by Bo and Liu" | [South China Morning Post, Choi Chi-yuk, 5 November 2012](https://www.scmp.com/news/china/article/1075158/party-plenum-promotes-two-generals-and-confirms-bo-xilai-expulsion) |
| Removal as NPC deputy | reported as 26 October 2012 | **n.a.** — not confirmed from a fetched page | — |

### Was a published disciplinary finding issued?

**Yes.** The Xinhua report of the 28 September 2012 Politburo decision published specific findings against Bo: abuse of power; "major responsibility in the Wang Lijun incident and the Bogu Kailai intentional homicide case"; taking "huge bribes"; "improper sexual relationships with a number of women"; and violations of organizational and personnel discipline ([China Daily / Xinhua](https://www.chinadaily.com.cn/china/2012-09/28/content_15791527.htm)).

### Did the mechanism match the Party Constitution then in force?

**Yes, on its face.** The CPC Constitution as amended at the 17th National Congress on 21 October 2007 — the version in force in September 2012 — provides at **Article 40**: "Any decision to remove a member or alternate member of the Central Committee … from his or her posts within the Party, to place such a person on probation within the Party or to expel such a person from the Party must be approved by a two thirds majority vote at a plenary meeting of the Party committee to which he or she belongs. In special circumstances, the decision may be taken first by the Standing Committee of the Political Bureau of the Central Committee … pending confirmation at the plenary meeting of the Party committee." It further provides: "A member or alternate member of the Central Committee who has seriously violated the criminal law shall be expelled from the Party on decision by the Political Bureau of the Central Committee." ([Constitution of the Communist Party of China, amended and adopted 21 October 2007, china.org.cn](http://www.china.org.cn/english/congress/229722.htm))

The observed sequence — Politburo decision 28 September 2012, plenum endorsement 4 November 2012 — maps onto the second sentence and onto the "pending confirmation at the plenary meeting" route. **Sharp contrast with Episode 1**, where the 1973 Constitution contained no such provision at all ([1973 CCP Constitution](https://www.marxists.org/subject/china/documents/cpc/CONSTITUTION_CPC.htm)).

The identical Article 40 text was carried into the version adopted 14 November 2012 ([Constitution of the CPC, adopted 14 November 2012, china.org.cn](http://www.china.org.cn/china/18th_cpc_congress/2012-11/16/content_27138030_8.htm)).

## 10. Did the transfers of the General Secretaryship and the state presidency follow the Party and state constitutions then in force?

### Party: General Secretary, November 2012 — YES

**Rule.** CPC Constitution (2007 version, in force at the time of the 18th Congress's opening): "**Article 22.** The Political Bureau, the Standing Committee of the Political Bureau and the General Secretary of the Central Committee of the Party are elected by the Central Committee in plenary session. The General Secretary of the Central Committee must be a member of the Standing Committee of the Political Bureau." ([china.org.cn, 2007 CPC Constitution](http://www.china.org.cn/english/congress/229722.htm)) Identical text in the [14 November 2012 version](http://www.china.org.cn/china/18th_cpc_congress/2012-11/16/content_27138030_4.htm).

**Practice.** Xi Jinping was elected General Secretary at the **1st Plenary Session of the 18th Central Committee on 15 November 2012**, attended by 205 Central Committee members and 171 alternates ([China Daily, 15 November 2012](https://www.chinadaily.com.cn/china/2012cpc/2012-11/15/content_15931883.htm); [Xinhua](http://www.xinhuanet.com/english/2017-10/23/c_136700371.htm)). Xi's own remarks that day confirm the plenum elected him ([USC US-China Institute transcript](https://china.usc.edu/node/21179)).

**Verdict: the constitutionally specified organ (a CC plenary session) performed the constitutionally specified act.** This is precisely what did *not* happen in October 1976.

### State: President, March 2013 — YES

**Rule.** PRC Constitution (text as amended 14 March 2004, the version in force in March 2013): **Article 62(4)** — the NPC has power "to elect the President and the Vice-President of the People's Republic of China"; **Article 62(6)** — "to elect the Chairman of the Central Military Commission and, upon nomination by the Chairman, to decide on the choice of all other members of the Central Military Commission" ([npc.gov.cn, PRC Constitution Art. 62](http://www.npc.gov.cn/zgrdw/englishnpc/Constitution/2007-11/15/content_1372965.htm)). **Article 79** — "The President and Vice-President of the People's Republic of China are elected by the National People's Congress. … The term of office of the President and Vice-President of the People's Republic of China is the same as that of the National People's Congress, and they shall serve no more than two consecutive terms." **Article 93** — "The term of office of the Central Military Commission is the same as that of the National People's Congress." ([Supreme People's Court English portal, "Constitution of the People's Republic of China (Full text after amendment on March 14, 2004)"](https://english.court.gov.cn/2015-07/17/c_1170165.htm))

**Practice.** Xi Jinping was elected President of the PRC and Chairman of the PRC Central Military Commission at the **4th plenary meeting of the 1st session of the 12th NPC on 14 March 2013**, by **2,952 votes for, 1 against, 3 abstentions** ([PRC Embassy to the US](https://us.china-embassy.gov.cn/eng/unshown/2013lianghui/201303/t20130314_5096928.htm); [china.org.cn](http://www.china.org.cn/china/NPC_CPPCC_2013/2013-03/14/content_28239654.htm)).

**Verdict: procedurally regular on the face of the constitutional text and the recorded vote.**

### Scholarly characterisation of procedural regularity

Alice Miller (Hoover Institution), *China Leadership Monitor* No. 40 — the strongest source found, and unambiguous:

- "The processes of generational turnover of China's leadership at the Chinese Communist Party's 18th National Congress extended patterns of formal politics that trace their roots to Deng Xiaoping's political reforms of the 1980s… As such, the transition in the party leadership at the 18th Congress marked another step forward in the **institutionalization** of Chinese leadership politics."
- "However much leadership combat preceded it, the 18th Congress convened **according to the prescribed five-year stipulation in the party constitution**."
- "This, together with the consistent convocation of Central Committee plenums and other subordinate institutions in the run-up to the congress, accords with the **fastidious adherence to formal party processes** characteristic of the entire post-Mao reform era."
- "The CCP leadership has delivered another **orderly and deliberate transfer of power** at the top, in which an established party chief has retired in favor of a younger leader prepared to succeed him."
- "As it had been in 2002 and 2007, the age 68 retirement norm was applied again to Politburo membership at the 18th Congress." — "Adherence to this norm across three party congresses confirms its consolidation."
- The 18th Congress convened 8–14 November 2012 and "following normal precedent, the newly elected 18th Central Committee held its First Plenum on the 15th."

([Alice Miller, *China Leadership Monitor* 40, Hoover Institution](https://www.hoover.org/sites/default/files/research/docs/CLM40AM.pdf))

**The one irregularity Miller does note is timing, not procedure:** "The convocation of the congress was late by the standard of previous congresses in the post-Mao era, which have usually met in September or October. Its dates replicate almost precisely the dates of the 2002 16th Party Congress … suggesting that leadership transitions of this scale provoke greater political contention and so incur delay." ([same](https://www.hoover.org/sites/default/files/research/docs/CLM40AM.pdf))

**No fetched source characterises the 2012–13 transition as procedurally irregular.** Recorded in Gaps §9.

## 11. Did the CMC Chairmanship transfer at the same time, and was that unusual?

**Yes, it transferred simultaneously — and the unusual thing was the *absence* of a delay, not its presence.**

- **Party CMC:** Hu Jintao "also relinquished his post as chair of the CMC to Xi, who until then had served as vice chairman of the commission", at the same time as stepping down as general secretary in November 2012 ([Britannica, "Hu Jintao"](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Hu-Jintao)).
- **State CMC:** Xi was elected Chairman of the PRC Central Military Commission by the NPC on 14 March 2013 ([PRC Embassy](https://us.china-embassy.gov.cn/eng/unshown/2013lianghui/201303/t20130314_5096928.htm); [china.org.cn](http://www.china.org.cn/china/NPC_CPPCC_2013/2013-03/14/content_28239654.htm)).

**The Jiang Zemin comparison — confirmed from two independent sources:**

- Alice Miller: "Institutionalization of orderly transfer of power at the top was arguably advanced with Hu Jintao's decision to retire concurrently as CMC chairman, the top military post in China's political order, and to give the post to Xi Jinping **immediately, rather than retaining it for two years as Jiang Zemin had done in 2002**." ([*China Leadership Monitor* 40](https://www.hoover.org/sites/default/files/research/docs/CLM40AM.pdf))
- Britannica: Hu "consolidated his power in **September 2004** when he was named chair of the CMC following Jiang's resignation" ([Britannica, "Hu Jintao"](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Hu-Jintao)) — i.e. roughly two years after Hu became general secretary in 2002.

**Interpretation for the rubric:** the simultaneous CMC handover was treated by a specialist analyst as an *advance* in institutionalisation relative to the 2002 precedent, not as an anomaly or a sign of irregularity ([*China Leadership Monitor* 40](https://www.hoover.org/sites/default/files/research/docs/CLM40AM.pdf)). Also note the constitutional consequence: because "the term of office of the Central Military Commission is the same as that of the National People's Congress" ([PRC Constitution Art. 93](https://english.court.gov.cn/2015-07/17/c_1170165.htm)), the state CMC chairmanship could not transfer before the NPC session of March 2013 in any event; the Party CMC chairmanship, which is not NPC-bound, transferred in November 2012.

## 12. Sourced allegations of military involvement in the 2012 transition

### (a) The Wang Lijun incident, February 2012 — WELL SOURCED, but it is a police/security matter, not a military one

**Standard of sourcing: official Chinese judicial and government statements, corroborated by multiple international wire services.** This is the strongest evidentiary standing of any allegation in this section.

Official account, from the Chengdu Intermediate People's Court verdict as reported by Xinhua: "Feeling that he was in danger, Wang entered the U.S. Consulate General in Chengdu without permission at **2:31 p.m. on February 6** and asked the U.S. side for political asylum." "Wang left the U.S. diplomatic mission of his own volition at **11:35 p.m. on February 7** after being persuaded by the Chinese side." "Wang, acting as a state functionary, left his post without permission and defected to a foreign country's consulate while performing his official duties. His behavior constituted the crime of defection and the circumstances were serious." ([China Daily / Xinhua, 24 September 2012](https://www.chinadaily.com.cn/china/2012-09/24/content_15777110.htm))

Sentence: "seven years in prison for the charge of bending the law for selfish ends, two years in prison and deprivation of his political rights for one year for the charge of defection, two years in prison for the power abuse charge and nine years in prison for the charge of bribe-taking … a combined punishment of **15 years** in prison" ([China Daily / Xinhua](https://www.chinadaily.com.cn/china/2012-09/24/content_15777110.htm)).

Wang's role is also recorded in the official disciplinary finding against Bo Xilai, which cites Bo's "major responsibility in the Wang Lijun incident" ([China Daily / Xinhua, 28 September 2012](https://www.chinadaily.com.cn/china/2012-09/28/content_15791527.htm)).

**Wang Lijun was Chongqing's vice-mayor and former public security chief, not a military officer.** Nothing in the fetched official record alleges PLA involvement.

### (b) March 2012 Beijing troop-movement and coup allegations — WEAKLY SOURCED AND REFUTED

**Standard of sourcing: anonymous microblog posts and overseas Chinese-language and Falun Gong–affiliated outlets. No named official, documentary, or on-the-ground journalistic confirmation was found.**

What was alleged: "rumours had been spread that military vehicles were on the streets of Beijing" ([BBC News, 31 March 2012](https://www.bbc.com/news/world-asia-china-17570005)); on Weibo, "chatter … that tanks were rolling through Beijing", plus claims of gunfire ([The Diplomat, "Coup Rumors in China", March 2012](https://thediplomat.com/2012/03/coup-rumors-in-china/)).

**Refutations and adverse indicators:**

1. **BBC:** "while there is **no evidence to substantiate** the rumours of a coup, the party is clearly upset." ([BBC News](https://www.bbc.com/news/world-asia-china-17570005))
2. **Photographic evidence shown to be recycled:** "images of the military that appeared in the *Epoch Times* that were purportedly taken this week were later **found to have been taken from a military website, and dated back to 2010**, when parade preparations were taking place." ([The Diplomat](https://thediplomat.com/2012/03/coup-rumors-in-china/))
3. **The Diplomat's own assessment:** "the chatter on the Weibo microblog that tanks were rolling through Beijing was **almost certainly nonsense**." ([The Diplomat](https://thediplomat.com/2012/03/coup-rumors-in-china/))
4. **Expert assessment — Minxin Pei**, described as a regular *The Diplomat* contributor: "It's very hard to stage coups in China without the direct involvement of the highest-ranking generals, who today are not closely tied with particular factions… The unit guarding the central leadership isn't controlled by the state security apparatus. It answers only to the person most trusted by the top leadership (his formal title is the director of Central Security Bureau). So I wouldn't take such rumors very seriously." ([The Diplomat](https://thediplomat.com/2012/03/coup-rumors-in-china/))
5. **State response:** "Chinese police have arrested six people and shut 16 websites" over the rumours; the State Internet Information Office said they had a "very bad influence on the public"; Sina Weibo and Tencent Weibo disabled commenting 31 March – 3 April 2012 ([BBC News](https://www.bbc.com/news/world-asia-china-17570005)).

**Adjudication for the rubric:** the March 2012 Beijing troop-movement / coup allegation rests on **anonymous social-media claims and at least one demonstrably misattributed photo set**, and was assessed as unsubstantiated by contemporaneous mainstream reporting and by a named academic commentator. It should be scored as **refuted / not corroborated**, at the weakest tier of sourcing. Note the asymmetry against Episode 1: there, the *Beijing Garrison* really was tasked to secure media and transport nodes, on the record of a specialist scholarly account ([Teiwes & Sun, p.359](https://www.bannedthought.net/China/MaoEra/GPCR/BourgeoisCommentary/China'sOctoberCoupThatEndedTheMaoistEra-Sun-Teiwes.pdf)); in 2012 the equivalent claim collapses on inspection.

# Gaps and failures

Numbered candidly. This section is as important as the findings.

1. **Mao's death date rests on Britannica alone** — the brief's designated last-resort source. I did not fetch a primary document (the Central Committee's death announcement, or a *Peking Review* issue of September 1976). Every other death date in Episode 1 §3 for Zhou Enlai, Kang Sheng and Zhu De **also rests on Britannica alone**. Only Dong Biwu's death is sourced to a Chinese official outlet. This is a systematic weakness in Episode 1 §3: four of five death dates are single-source and from the weakest permitted source tier.

2. **Politburo size conflict.** English Wikipedia's "10th Central Committee" page states a "25-member Politburo", contradicting the official gov.cn record (21 full + 4 alternate) and the enumerated 1973 press communiqué. I resolved this in favour of the official and primary sources, but I did not re-fetch the Wikipedia page in this session to quote it exactly, so the conflict is recorded from an earlier reading rather than a citable fetch.

3. **The gov.cn phrasing is ambiguous on its own.** "Elected 9 Politburo Standing Committee members, 21 Politburo members, and 4 alternate members" is capable of an additive reading (9+21+4 = 34). The question of whether the PSC sits inside the 21 is only resolved by cross-checking the enumerated name lists in the 1973 press communiqué. Anyone applying a rubric to the gov.cn number alone could get this wrong.

4. **The executing unit of the Gang of Four arrests is NOT confirmed.** The brief asked me to confirm or refute the Central Guard Regiment / Unit 8341. Result: **partially supported, not confirmed.** Teiwes and Sun say the arrest was "organized by Wang Dongxing" without naming a unit; Hsü says Wang Dongxing commanded "the 20,000-man 8341 special unit". No fetched page states that Unit 8341 or the Central Guard Regiment made the arrests. Several promising sources were identified but **not fetched** in this session: a Wu De memoir excerpt and a piece on the 8341 unit hosted on Robert Suettinger's site, a FAS/IRP page on Unit 8341, and the *China Quarterly* article "Hua Kuo-feng and the Arrest of the 'Gang of Four'". Their URLs are listed at the end as *unfetched leads*, clearly separated.

5. **Major unresolved conflict between two academic sources on the arrest itself.** Teiwes & Sun (University of Sydney / Monash, specialist archival work) versus Hsü (*China Without Mao*, Oxford University Press) disagree on:
   - **Date and time:** ~8 p.m. on **6 October** (Teiwes & Sun, p.360) versus a plot meeting "in the early morning of October 5" and the Four invited "to attend an emergency Politburo meeting at **midnight, October 5**" (Hsü).
   - **Violence:** Hsü states "Wang Hongwen arrived first. He resisted arrest and killed two guards but was himself wounded and subdued." Teiwes & Sun's account contains no such episode and describes the whole operation as taking "no more than 35 minutes".
   - **Chen Xilian's role:** Hsü calls him "Beijing Garrison Commander"; Teiwes & Sun identify Wu Zhong as Garrison Commander and place Chen Xilian's authority in the Military Affairs Commission.
   - **Military dispositions:** Hsü's Great Wall guard, transfer of "another regiment … to watch over the 38th regiment", and two Guangzhou divisions on airlift standby have no counterpart in Teiwes & Sun.
   
   I have **not** adjudicated this conflict. My inclination is to weight Teiwes & Sun higher for the operational detail (they engage directly with the rumour evidence, name their sourcing chains, and explicitly dismiss specific claims), and to note that Hsü's chapter dates to a 1990 online publication of a book edition and is more narrative in register. **But that is my judgement, not a sourced finding**, and a rubric relying on the arrest chronology should treat the date as contested.

6. **No scholarly source defending the legality of the October 1976 arrests was found.** The brief asked for quotations "on BOTH sides of that question if both exist". On the fetched evidence they do not: the range runs from "inherently illegal" (Teiwes & Sun) through "dubious legality … the party and the country were willing to overlook the legal formality" (Hsü) to "usurped the power by military force" (Chien Liu). The closest thing to a legitimacy defence is Teiwes & Sun's observation that the elite "quite gratefully accepted the actions and legitimacy of the new formal leader" — which is about *acceptance*, not *legality*. I cannot rule out that such a source exists; I can only report that I did not find one. Also note I did not search Chinese-language official historiography, which would be the most likely place to find an official legality claim.

7. **The 38th Army question is effectively unanswered.** Neither strong source addresses a "38th Army". Hsü mentions a "38th **regiment**" — a different-sized formation — in a passage otherwise unsupported by Teiwes & Sun. A separately identified US government translation series (JPRS-CAR-90-005, hosted at DTIC) reportedly mentions the 38th Group Army at Baoding and the arrest of a Deputy Political Commissar Zhang Qiuqiao, but **I did not fetch it in this session**, so nothing from it is asserted above. Listed as an unfetched lead.

8. **Whether the 17th Politburo had zero alternates is n.a.** I found no positive statement. The official name list omits alternates and CRS notes the 16th had one, but absence of mention is not proof of absence.

9. **Only one source characterises the 2012–13 transition's procedural regularity.** Alice Miller's *China Leadership Monitor* piece is a strong specialist source, but it is a **single** source carrying the whole of §10's scholarly characterisation, and Hoover's China Leadership Monitor is analytical commentary rather than peer-reviewed scholarship. I found **no** source arguing the transition was procedurally irregular, which may reflect genuine scholarly consensus or may reflect the limits of my search. A rubric should not treat "no dissent found" as "no dissent exists".

10. **Bo Xilai plenum ratification date: sources conflict.** The SCMP report dated 5 November 2012 says the plenum "wrapped up a four-day closed-door conclave yesterday", implying **4 November 2012**, and the SCMP page itself does **not** state the plenum's dates. Earlier in this research I encountered English Wikipedia pages giving inconsistent dates — "Bo Xilai" indicating 4 November and "Bo Xilai incident" indicating 7 November. I did not obtain an official Xinhua or CPC communiqué stating the 7th plenum's dates, so **the exact ratification date is confirmed only by inference from SCMP's "yesterday"**. Recorded as 4 November 2012 with that caveat.

11. **Bo Xilai's removal as an NPC deputy is n.a.** Reported elsewhere as 26 October 2012 by the NPC Standing Committee, but I did not fetch a page stating it, so it is omitted from the findings.

12. **Britannica's account of Xi's election is inaccurate and conflicts with the official record.** Britannica says "the **party congress** elected Xi Jinping to the office" of general secretary ([Britannica, "Hu Jintao"](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Hu-Jintao)). The official Xinhua/China Daily record and the CPC Constitution both establish that the general secretary is elected by the **Central Committee in plenary session**, and that Xi was elected at the 1st Plenary Session of the 18th CC on 15 November 2012, not by the Congress. I relied on Britannica only for the CMC dates in §11, where Miller independently corroborates the substance. But this is a live example of the last-resort source being wrong on a procedural point that matters to the rubric.

13. **Chen Yonggui's date of loss of his Politburo seat is n.a.** Deng's August 1980 document confirms only the Vice-Premiership. Search snippets (not fetched pages) variously indicate a September 1980 resignation and retention of Politburo membership until 1982. Since these were not fetched, no date is asserted. What *is* established from a fetched source is that the loss of the state post occurred in 1980, i.e. **after** the October 1978 window boundary.

14. **Li Desheng's January 1975 demotion sits awkwardly in the window definition.** It occurred inside ~Sept 1974 – Oct 1978 and was a loss of PSC membership and a CC vice-chairmanship, but **not** a removal from the Politburo. A rubric counting "Politburo members removed" must decide explicitly whether intra-Politburo demotion counts; the source records it as an approved request to be relieved, not a purge.

15. **No Wilson Center Digital Archive or Cold War International History Project document was obtained** for either episode, despite the brief's preference for them. My searches did not surface a usable 1976 document from those collections. Likewise no *China Quarterly*, *China Journal*, or *Journal of Contemporary China* article was successfully fetched in full — the *China Quarterly* article most directly on point sits behind Cambridge Core.

16. **Single-source dependencies, listed plainly:**
    - Politburo full-member enumeration and the PSC-inside-21 finding: **one primary document** (the 1973 China Reconstructs supplement), with the aggregate numbers independently confirmed by gov.cn.
    - All 1973 Constitution textual findings: **one hosted text** (marxists.org), though the same text also appears in the China Reconstructs PDF, which is a partial independent check.
    - The entire refutation of the Mao Yuanxin Shenyang mobilisation: **one source** (Teiwes & Sun), whose own evidence is an unnamed Party historian relaying Li Desheng's denial — and which leaves Sun Yuguo's subsequent removal from command unexplained.
    - The Beijing Garrison tasking in October 1976: **one source** (Teiwes & Sun).
    - Hua's July 1977 CC confirmation and the Gang of Four's July 1977 expulsion: **one source** (gov.cn).
    - Miller's *China Leadership Monitor* carries §10 and the comparative judgement in §11 largely alone.
    - Chien Liu's *Modern China Studies* article is a journal of modest standing relative to the others; I cite it only for the existence of a "usurpation" characterisation, not for any factual claim.

17. **Weak-standing sources relied on anywhere in the findings:** Britannica (four death dates, plus CMC dates in §11); bannedthought.net as the *host* of the Teiwes & Sun paper (the paper itself is scholarly, but I read it via a partisan-archive mirror rather than the journal of record, and I did not verify the mirror against the published version); The Diplomat and BBC News as journalism rather than scholarship for §12 — appropriate for adjudicating a rumour's evidentiary standing, but journalism nonetheless; SCMP for the plenum date by inference.

18. **Teiwes & Sun affiliations were confirmed from a different publication** (a 2022 University of Sydney China Studies Centre working paper) than the paper quoted. The affiliations are theirs, but they are not stated on the fetched copy of the October Coup paper itself.
