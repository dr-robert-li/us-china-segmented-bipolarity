# Two Ledgers: Why the Argument About China's Trajectory Cannot Be Settled by Adding Up

## 1. Two ledgers, both real

Begin with the collision, because the collision is the finding.

One ledger reads like the file on a declining power. Official growth fell from about 14 per cent in 2007 to about 6 per cent in 2019, and the studies the sceptics cite as rigorous put the true rate closer to 2. Total factor productivity declined 1.3 per cent a year on average from 2008 to 2019, on Conference Board figures. Capital efficiency deteriorated to the point where it takes three times as many inputs to produce a unit of growth as it did in the early 2000s. Total debt surged eight-fold between 2008 and 2019 and passed 300 per cent of GDP. And the demographic projection runs one direction only: between 2020 and 2050, China will lose some 200 million working-age adults and gain 200 million pensioners, with medical and social-security spending required to triple as a share of GDP, from 10 to 30 per cent, to meet obligations already incurred. Nothing in this column is contested by the people who compile the other one.

The other ledger, drawn from largely the same decade, reads like the file on an ascending one. Electricity demand of 10,573 TWh in 2025 -- roughly a third of world demand, nearly double the 5,802 TWh of 2015. Fifty-eight per cent of the world's solar installations and 72 per cent of its wind in a single year. Coal's share of generation down from 70 per cent in 2015 to 54 per cent in 2025, with fossil generation falling in absolute terms for the first time in a decade. Eighty-eight billion dollars of transmission and distribution investment in one year, against roughly 400 billion spent on grids worldwide. A robot stock of 2,027,000 units -- 43 per cent of the world's, doubled within three years -- absorbing 54 per cent of a year's global installations, while domestic suppliers' share of their home market moved from 47 to 57 per cent in a single year. Nothing in this column is contested either.

It matters that these are not two evidentiary worlds. The robot census showing the installation surge is published by the International Federation of Robotics, the same body whose data underwrite the automation-overcapacity critique; the productivity decline is a Conference Board series sitting in the same analytical apparatus as the electricity figures the optimists quote from Ember and the IEA. Each camp cites the other's sources, for other purposes, without embarrassment. There is no factual dispute here worth the name. A reader who has followed the China debate for a decade has watched both columns lengthen simultaneously, and has watched each camp treat its own column as the signal and the other's as noise.

One detail from the second ledger previews the essay's method. China's 54 per cent share of a year's robot installations sits against a 43 per cent share of the operating stock. Flow share above stock share means the stock share is still converging upward: a single-year snapshot understates the derivative. Level, share, and rate of change are three different facts about the same series, and a verdict that quotes one of them has chosen. The choosing is the subject of this essay.

So the opening move is to refuse the reconciliation. Anyone who reads these two columns and reports a single directional verdict -- rising, peaking, declining -- has done something that deserves to be named. They have applied an exchange rate between ledgers: so many points of productivity decline per terawatt-hour, so many pension liabilities per robot. The verdict is not in either ledger. It is in the exchange rate. And the exchange rate is almost never shown, almost never argued for, and in most published instances was never deliberately chosen at all -- it fell out of a normalisation routine, a weighting default, an index formula inherited from a codebook written for other wars. Two analysts who share every number above and differ in their verdicts differ, arithmetically, in nothing but the exchange rate. The rest of the essay is about what that fact does, and does not, license.

## 2. What an index does when you are not looking

The composite-indicator literature is a mature field with theorems, and almost nobody in the power-measurement debate has read it. This movement states what it establishes, for a reader who has never opened that literature and will not open it after.

Start with the result that should end most arguments about "importance". In an additive aggregation -- a weighted sum, which is what nearly every power index is -- the weights are substitution rates, not importances. A weight of 0.2 on energy and 0.1 on demography does not say energy matters twice as much; it says the index will trade one unit of demographic decline for half a unit of energy growth, at every margin, for every country, forever. The field's own handbook, produced jointly by the OECD and the European Commission's Joint Research Centre, states it directly: "In both linear and geometric aggregations, weights express trade-offs between indicators", and there is "an inconsistency between how weights are conceived ... and the actual meaning". Munda and Nardo prove the incompatibility rather than asserting it: symmetrical importance "is incompatible with a linear aggregation rule"; the estimation of weights "is equivalent to that of substitution rates, implying a compensatory logic"; the trade-offs "depend on the scales of measurement"; and therefore the interpretation of weights as importance "is always completely inappropriate". This is a theorem, not a complaint. Every additive index asserts a full schedule of trade-offs its authors never argued for, and most authors do not know their instrument is asserting it.

What do the implied trade-offs look like when someone computes them? Ravallion did it for the Human Development Index: the HDI's implicit valuation of a year of human life runs from about 0.50 dollars a year in Zimbabwe to almost 9,000 dollars a year in the richest countries -- a four-order-of-magnitude spread in the price of the same good, inside one instrument, invisible to every user who reads only the rankings. His general charge is the one that transfers to power measurement: the weights on components are usually explicit, the weights on the underlying dimensions are not, and "little or no attention is given to the implied tradeoffs". Sensitivity analysis of composite rankings exposes the same pathology from the other side: under defensible alternative assumptions about normalisation and weighting, Luxembourg can place anywhere from 3rd to 113th, and in one environmental performance index, 60 of 163 country rankings depend on the assumptions rather than the data. The rankings feel like measurements. They are, in substantial part, decisions.

Normalisation deserves its own paragraph, because it is where aggregation hides. Whether components are scaled by z-scores, min-max ranges, or system shares changes which component dominates the sum, so the choice carries part of the weighting invisibly. The handbook's demonstration is as simple as temperature: express the same physical series in Celsius or in Fahrenheit and a min-max normalised composite built on it can reorder the results. For capability measurement the point is not a curiosity. Generation capacity, compute throughput, and market depth have no common scale; some normalisation must be imposed before they can be summed; and the imposition is doing part of the aggregation's work where no reader looks for it. An index whose verdicts do not survive its own choice of units is not measuring. It is voting.

There is a limit case at the end of this road, and it is Arrow's. Aggregating multiple orderings into one, under conditions each of which sounds innocuous, cannot be done without either intransitivity or a dictator. The handbook invokes the theorem by name -- "no perfect aggregation convention can exist" -- and converts it into a working constraint: no indicator or dimension above 50 per cent of total weight, or that indicator "would become a dictator in Arrow's terminology". The theorem does not say composites are useless. It says every composite has chosen a violation, and the choice is an editorial act.

Now hold the field's instruments up to this light, using only what the instruments say about themselves. The Correlates of War capability index -- six components, equal weights, more than a thousand peer-reviewed studies -- averages system shares, and its own codebook concedes the score "could then be computed on as few as one component". The Lowy Asia Power Index does what almost no one does: it publishes its weights -- Economic Capability 17.5 per cent, Military Capability 17.5, Resilience 10, Future Resources 10, Economic Relationships 15, and 10 each for Defence Networks, Diplomatic Influence and Cultural Influence -- concedes that "it is of course possible to reach other value judgements", and then reports that its own sensitivity analysis found the number of indicators "quantitatively more important than our weighting scheme". That is a publisher volunteering that the visible dial is not the one that steers. And Global Firepower, the index journalists reach for first, maintains a formula page that, at the time the source file for this essay was assembled, returned a 404.

None of this is a gotcha delivered from outside; it is the field grading its own instruments. The most authoritative recent statement is a 2026 methods review concluding that "the notion of a single aggregate quantity of national power existing in the abstract ... makes little or no sense" and that aggregated measures "select and weight variables in an essentially arbitrary way". When the practice's own methodological literature has reached that verdict, continuing the practice is a choice. Sometimes -- the next movement insists on this -- a defensible one. But a choice, not a measurement.

## 3. The strongest argument against everything in Movement 2

Here is the movement a hostile reader should find fairest, placed before any conclusion is drawn from Movement 2, because if it cannot be stated at full strength the essay has no business existing.

Scalar reduction keeps working. Beckley's two-variable product -- GDP times GDP per capita, a deliberate crudity -- predicts 78 per cent of wars and 70 per cent of militarised interstate disputes over 1816--2010. The six-component index, with its codebook and its thousand studies, manages 70 and 64; GDP alone manages 68 and 64. Power transition theory ran for decades on a single GNP proxy, and its architects reported that the "parsimonious and robust measure performed as well as the more complex index of power" they might have built instead. A modern replication cuts deeper still: decomposed out of sample, a single component of the six -- the energy measure -- carries 16.8 per cent of predictive importance, while the whole aggregate improves on a naive predictor by 1.2 per cent, a finding its authors summarise by calling the aggregation "entirely ad hoc". Note carefully what that last result argues for. Not a vector. Fewer variables. One, perhaps.

So the honest statement of the record is this: in the one arena where power measures have ever been scored -- out-of-sample prediction of conflict outcomes across two centuries -- adding components did not help, and multiplying two variables did. Anyone proposing a four-element capability vector with per-element posteriors, as the research programme behind this essay does, is proposing an instrument that has never outperformed a simpler one on any historical test. And the disadvantage is not temporary. The vector's claims run to 2030, 2040, 2075; the scoreboard for them arrives after the claims have ceased to matter. The two-variable scalar has a validation record. The vector cannot acquire one within the lifetime of the claim.

The programme's answer -- that prediction of war outcomes is not the question, that the two-ledger question is about which domains are moving in which directions, and that a scalar optimised for the first question destroys the information the second is about -- appears in Movement 7, where its costs are also billed. It is recorded here only so the reader knows the essay is aware of it. It does not neutralise the objection, because the objection includes the possibility that the finer-grained question cannot be answered at all, by anyone, on any timescale that matters.

The essay does not resolve this. It states the ground on which it could be resolved -- an out-of-sample record for domain-wise trajectory claims, scored per domain, accumulated over decades -- and concedes that the ground lies decades away. The bet has been placed without waiting. The reader is entitled to know that, and now does.

## 4. Nesting rather than refuting

The peaking-power thesis deserves to be treated as the best-specified position in the debate, because it is. Its strongest published form states a case-selection rule with unusual precision: every case from 1870 to 2018 in which a great power's per-capita GDP grew at least twice as fast as the global average for at least seven years and then suffered at least a 50 per cent decline in growth rates over the next seven -- a rule that yields nine cases after wartime downturns are excluded, three of which are then set aside as overdetermined. Add a named behavioural mechanism -- the closing-window logic under which a state that believes its position has peaked becomes more willing to run risks now -- and named conditioning variables that say when the mechanism should and should not fire, and you have what a specified claim looks like: a reader can list what would count against it.

The contrast case shows how rare that is. The Thucydides Trap file claims immunity from selection bias on the ground that it covers "the entire universe of the cases" -- and concedes, in the same document, that the file "is open". It offers no operational definition of a rising or a ruling power. The consequence is not rhetorical: applying defensible alternative case definitions moves the headline war rate from 12 of 16 to 19 of 30, and to 1 of 8 for cases after 1950 -- which is the population that matters for the present question. A case file that cannot say what would have excluded a case cannot say what its rate is a rate of.

And now the symmetry this essay owes its reader, in the same breath and not in a footnote. The peaking thesis's load-bearing evidence, after its own exclusions, is five to six cases. The research programme behind this essay rests its priors on five to eight historical analogues. These are the same order of magnitude, and the sample-size critique of one is a sample-size critique of the other. Any reader who finds nine cases thin should find eight cases thin. The difference the programme can claim is procedural, not evidential: its analogue list was committed in advance under a stated selection discipline, and the analogues inform priors that data are permitted to overrule, rather than conclusions asserted directly. Whether that procedural difference is worth anything is a fair question, and Movement 5 is about the machinery that at least makes it checkable.

The substantive move, then, is not refutation. The programme treats peaking as a region of a parameter space: a trajectory in which the challenger's relative position rises, peaks inside the window, and gives back a defined amount is one of five regimes the model can return, with prior mass assigned and a falsifiable footprint. If the peaking theorists are right, the machinery is built to say so, and to say so against thresholds fixed before the data arrived. This is nesting, not charity, and it cuts in both directions -- because the peaking literature's own internal critics already point at the vector. One asks whether peak China is "an absolute term or a relative one -- and if the latter, relative to what?", observes that "China could peak in one area but advance in others, complicating the calculation", and adds that economic peaking is not geopolitical peaking, "a distinction lost on many advocates". That is this essay's opening collision restated as a hypothesis.

Another critic reaches the same place from the opposite direction, and does the empirical work that makes the objection quantitative. Lind validates candidate power metrics against historian-generated great-power lists for 1820--1990 -- which states did historians actually treat as great powers, and what did those states measure at the time -- and derives normal ranges. The result dissolves the overtaking question rather than answering it: China already stands at 130 per cent of the leading state's GDP against a great-power historical normal range of 17--45 per cent, at 36 per cent on the GDP-times-GDP-per-capita composite against a normal range of 8--28, and at 32 per cent on military expenditure against a range of 23--105. On some yardsticks the overtaking happened years ago; on others it is nowhere in sight; and the yardstick with the best predictive record in the field draws her bluntest verdict -- GDP per capita "is not a sound metric for this purpose", showing "significant overlap among great powers, middle powers, and all non-great powers", and offering "a national average, which obscures a country's highest level of technological performance". Her conclusion -- "'catching up' or 'overtaking' are the wrong benchmarks" -- is an objection lodged against trajectory framing as such, and therefore against this programme's regime taxonomy too. It is recorded here as an objection the programme has not answered, rather than one it has.

## 5. Saying in advance what would make you wrong

Pre-registration is a discipline from the experimental sciences, largely unused in security studies, and the claim this movement makes for it is deliberately modest, because the immodest claims are false.

The purpose is narrow, and the source that states it most carefully should be quoted rather than paraphrased: "Preregistration distinguishes analyses and outcomes that result from predictions from those that result from postdictions", and -- decisively -- "Preregistration does not favor prediction over postdiction; its purpose is to make clear which is which". The same authors concede that "there is not yet sufficient experimental evidence establishing its superiority for reproducibility". The garden of forking paths that makes the distinction urgent is quantifiable: in Olken's accounting, a ten-table analysis tree already implies 3^10 -- 59,049 -- possible regressions, and real pre-analysis plans attempting to close such gardens have exceeded fifty pages. An analyst choosing after seeing the data can walk that garden toward any verdict while sincerely believing each step was principled. Registration closes the garden before the data are seen. That is all it does.

The objections deserve their full weight, and three of them bite this programme specifically. Full pre-specification is infeasible -- 59,049 branches is the argument *against* plans as much as for them -- so the programme registered falsification conditions and thresholds rather than a full analysis tree, which is a weaker commitment and is described as such. Pre-specification "prevents you from learning about your data as you analyze it"; that cost is accepted, not denied. With historical data, "a scholar may have glimpsed the data before the study registration", so registration "cannot send as clear a signal" -- true of this programme's 2026 baseline, which uses published historical series, and not true of its 2030--2050 conditions, whose outcomes do not exist for anyone to have glimpsed. That last asymmetry is the entire argument for pre-registering a long-horizon forecast, and it is one sentence long: the glimpsing objection cannot apply to a value that does not exist yet.

There is also a jurisdictional objection, and it concedes the most interesting ground. Reviewing pre-analysis plans across economics, Coffman and Niederle find they "have quite limited upside" in most settings and "may discourage the use of novel research designs" -- but concede that the value concentrates in "costly, one-of-a-kind" studies that cannot be rerun. Monogan's classification for political science points the same way: studies of existing information and economic time series are low-value candidates for registration; a researcher who "plans to study an upcoming election" is the high-value case, because the outcome does not yet exist. A fifty-year single-run forecast of a two-state trajectory is about as one-of-a-kind and as unrerunnable as a design can be, and its registered conditions are all, in Monogan's sense, upcoming elections. On the objectors' own accounting, this is the rare class where the discipline pays.

The deepest objection is empirical: registration has not been shown to make findings more accurate, and the largest social-science forecasting tournament to date is a bath of cold water for the whole genre. Social scientists' forecasts "were on average no more accurate than those of simple statistical models"; the experts beat all three naive benchmarks in one of twelve domains; and their "subjective confidence in their forecasts was not related to the accuracy of their estimates". A pre-registered wrong answer is still wrong. The discipline buys auditability, not validity, and this essay claims only the first. The programme drew two operational consequences from the tournament rather than arguing with it: a naive statistical benchmark is mandatory beside every reported trajectory, and confidence statements are never offered as evidence of reliability.

What auditability looks like in practice is the concrete instance this movement exists for. The programme committed eight falsification conditions -- thresholds, evaluation windows, named data sources, adjudication rules -- before any estimation code existed, with a commit history establishing the order. The resulting table is unflattering, and its unflattering rows are the evidence of function. Two of the eight sit within roughly a percentage point of their thresholds: one at about 0.1 points from a 6.0 per cent threshold, on a value that is an estimate rather than an outturn and so cannot yet trigger; the other about 1.1 points below a 20 per cent threshold, measured on a proxy for estimated compute with its tolerance band not yet established. Those two nearest conditions are also the two least directly measured -- the conditions carrying the most weight are the ones most exposed to definitional choices, and the programme's own log says exactly that: "That is not a well-calibrated falsifier set, and the papers should say so." Meanwhile two conditions cannot presently resolve at all -- one because the publication its rule counts does not exist in the required shape, one because the series it needs is unavailable -- and a third has no formal adjudication yet and is labelled provisional. Calibration exercises against the historical record made things more uncomfortable, not less: of the three paired discrimination tests that could be run, one condition passed, one passed only generically, and one **failed outright**, returning the same verdict on the archetypal positive case and the negative control -- a demonstrated false-negative mode now flagged on every future verdict from that condition. All of this is dated, committed, and cannot now be removed. A reader in 2030 will be able to check what this programme said would count as being wrong, and whether it flinched. That is the entire content of the claim -- and against the tournament evidence above, it had better be, because nothing in the record entitles anyone to the stronger one.

## 6. The model rejected its own priors

This movement exists for the sceptical reader who suspects, reasonably, that a Bayesian apparatus with authored priors is a machine for laundering opinions. The most useful thing the programme can show that reader is the day the machine said no.

Five prior-predictive gates were specified before any prior was written: checks that the model, fed only its priors and no data, can reach every regime it claims to distinguish, at the horizons where it claims them; that the thesis regime can be driven to low mass by some admissible data, so the model is capable of disconfirming its own programme; that no trajectories are physically absurd; that the structure is exactly symmetric under exchange of the two states; and that no divergence regime dominates before any evidence arrives. Failure of any one blocks estimation.

Two of the five rejected the committed priors. The dual-constraint regime -- both states materially bound by their own structural limits -- carried 0.025 prior mass at the 2050 horizon against a required floor of 0.05: the model as configured could barely produce one of the five futures it advertises. And the trajectory-sanity gate failed harder: the committed priors produced a 2050 input multiple of 892.7 against a pre-registered ceiling of 20, with 5.8 per cent of draws breaching -- worlds containing, at the tail, a national transmission fleet hundreds of times its 2026 size within twenty-four years. Estimation was blocked. Nothing had been fitted; no data had been seen; the machine was stopped by its own pre-committed checks. The failing configuration was committed as-is rather than replaced, so that whatever amendment followed could be read against it.

Three details make this passage load-bearing rather than merely candid. First: the run log recording the coverage failure lists the candidate remedies and names which one would pass the gate most cheaply -- names it *as the temptation*, in the file, so that if a later amendment reached for it, the reach would be visible against a dated record of the incentive. The remedy eventually adopted was different in kind: the output contract was restricted -- the barely-reachable regime is now reported only at the 2075 horizon, where the committed priors can actually populate it -- on the stated ground that a posterior for a regime at a horizon the prior structure cannot inform is a number with no content, true whatever any gate says.

The checking code itself needed checking, and the record says so. The first implementation of the symmetry gate consumed different random numbers in the base and mirrored runs, so the comparison measured Monte Carlo noise as well as asymmetry -- and failed on noise, indicting the structure for a defect that was in the test. The fix, pre-drawn shock streams applied identically to both runs, makes the expected deviation exactly zero, so that any deviation at all is structural. A symmetry test that cannot distinguish asymmetry from its own randomness is worse than none, because it manufactures grounds for rebuilding a sound structure; the episode is in the run log because the next implementer of such a test will otherwise repeat it.

Second, and more instructive: an earlier gate run produced a plausible structural diagnosis -- a quantitative, well-formed story about a saturation limitation of the model -- that turned out to be an artefact. An audit found the sampler had substituted surrogates for four committed prior distributions; the diagnosis was an artefact of one of the substitutions; and acting on it would have widened a pre-registered prior in exactly the direction that made the failing gate pass. No gate could have caught this, because every gate consumes the sampler's output and inherits its errors. It was caught by an audit comparing the sampler's output, distribution by distribution, against the committed text -- now a standing test. This is the failure mode a pre-registration regime is least protected against: the registered object was correct, the code implementing it was not, and the code's error generated a plausible argument for amending the registered object. A wrong finding that looked exactly like a right one is the failure mode that should worry readers of every quantitative claim in this debate -- including this programme's -- and the only known defence is the boring one: audits against commitments, not confidence.

Third: the pass, when it came, is reported with its margins. At the amended configuration all five gates pass -- and the passing run's record states that the segmentation regime's coverage sits at 0.070 against the 0.05 floor and dips below it under one sensitivity setting of the classifier constants, whose gate-flipping values were computed, published, and then frozen, precisely so that a later reader can check that none was quietly adjusted. The trajectory tail still reaches 29 times the base-year level inside its permitted breach rate. These are honest margins, not comfortable ones.

The general point, stated without moralising: these failures are of the intended kind. A framework that had passed all five gates on the first attempt would have told us less about itself -- a pass can mean sound structure or toothless gates, and only a failure record distinguishes the two. The gates do not certify the model. They certify that the structure was interrogated before data arrived, and that estimation stayed blocked for exactly as long as the interrogation kept finding problems.

## 7. What a vector costs, and what it refuses

Close on the price, because the payoff has had six movements.

The programme maintains four capability elements with posterior distributions per element. It emits no cross-state scalar ratio -- there is no headline number in which China stands at 0.87 of the United States or 1.13 of it. Composites are permitted only with full weighting-sensitivity analysis published beside them. The costs of this discipline are not decorative, and there are three.

It does not answer the field's central question. Asked "is the challenger overtaking, and when", the programme returns four trajectories and a refusal. Power transition theory staked its empirical claim on a single contingency table -- twenty dyad-periods over 1860--1980, the signature cell of parity-plus-transition splitting five wars against five non-wars -- and a vector cannot populate that table at all, because populating it requires exactly the exchange rate the vector declines to set. It forfeits the one validation scalar indices have: the predictive record of Movement 3 belongs to the scalars, and nothing the vector produces can be scored against it for decades. And the discipline is imperfect from the inside: one of the four elements -- net capacity after production, welfare and security deductions -- is a composite in all but name, requiring exactly the trade-off judgements Movement 2 objects to in others. The author of the best-performing scalar concedes of his own index that it is "a primitive proxy" for net resources; the same concession applies to this programme's net element with the same force. The difference is of degree and of disclosure, not of kind. Anyone who finds that unsatisfying is reading correctly.

A field that has documented every property in Movement 2 and continued the practice for a thousand studies does not have an information problem. It has an alternatives problem: the scalar answers a question -- who is stronger, by how much -- that nothing else on offer answers at all, and any proposal that removes the scalar owes the field an account of what happens to that question. The programme's account is the honest and unsatisfying one: for some purposes the question has no well-posed answer, and the felt precision of the existing answers is supplied by the arithmetic, not the evidence. Readers who need the ranking anyway -- for force planning, for alliance accounting -- will keep using the scalars, and the predictive record in Movement 3 says the crude ones serve those purposes about as well as anything. The vector is not for those purposes. It is for the narrower question the scalars cannot carry: which domains are moving in which directions, with what confidence, and whether the pattern matches any of five pre-committed structural stories.

What the refusal actually refuses is narrower than "aggregation", and more defensible. It refuses to convert measurement disagreement into substantive disagreement. Return to the two ledgers. Two analysts holding identical data and different exchange rates will report opposite verdicts on China's trajectory -- and will describe themselves, in print and at conferences, as disagreeing about China. They are mostly not. They are disagreeing about arithmetic conventions, and the conventions are doing the work their evidence cannot. The dashboard alternative comes from inside the composite-indicator literature, not from this programme: "nagging doubts remain about the value-added of mashup indices ... relative to the 'dashboard' alternative of monitoring the components separately". A dashboard with per-element uncertainty and pre-registered failure conditions is what the programme's vector amounts to. It is less satisfying than a number. It is also the only reporting convention under which the two analysts' actual disagreement, if any survives the accounting, becomes visible -- because it is the only one that shows the exchange rate as a choice rather than burying it in a formula.

End on a caution rather than a preference, so the essay does not close by admiring itself. Measurement error, ignored, does not merely blur findings; it manufactures them. When measurement uncertainty in democracy scores was carried through a standard downstream regression, explanatory power fell from .63 on the raw scores, to .57 on posterior means, to .40 with uncertainty fully propagated -- and a quadratic term that had been treated as a substantive finding became indistinguishable from zero. Ignoring the error had not widened the intervals; it had created a result. Capability measurement is built on national statistics whose definitions differ across the two states in question -- energy accounting boundaries, robot-stock definitions, financial aggregates -- and most published power indices report no uncertainty at all. The prudent inference is not that the indices are wrong. It is that nobody currently knows which of their findings would survive honest error bars -- and that the two-ledger stalemate this essay opened with may itself be partly an artefact of treating uncertain measurements as exact. The programme behind this essay will publish its error bars. That is not a promise of being right. It is a commitment to being checkable, which is the only one on offer.
