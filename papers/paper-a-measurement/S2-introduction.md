# Paper A, section 2 -- Introduction: the problem the field has, stated with its own literature

**Status.** Draft, 2026-08-20. The section's argumentative burden is deliberately inverted: it does not assert that scalar power indices are bad, it shows that the field's own practitioners say so, in print, while continuing to use them -- and then concedes the two hardest facts against the paper's own position before proceeding.

---

## 2.1 The practice

The Composite Index of National Capability aggregates six components -- total population, urban population, iron and steel production, primary energy consumption, military personnel, military expenditure -- as an unweighted mean of system shares. It is currently at version 7.0, covering 1816--2022 ([Correlates of War](https://correlatesofwar.org/data-sets/national-material-capabilities/)). The equal weighting is not an interpretation imposed by critics; the codebook states it, and states further that the index "could then be computed on as few as one component" ([NMC v5 codebook](https://correlatesofwar.org/wp-content/uploads/NMC_Documentation_v5_0.pdf)). More than 1,000 peer-reviewed studies have used CINC to measure power ([Beckley, *International Security* 43(2)](https://direct.mit.edu/isec/article-abstract/43/2/7/12211)).

## 2.2 The practitioners' own verdict

The critique of this practice is not external. It is the field grading its own instrument, in its own journals:

- Carroll and Kenkel find the equal weighting "is entirely ad hoc", observe that the index "assigns the same importance to military spending as it does to personal energy consumption", and measure the payoff: capability ratios improve out-of-sample prediction of dispute outcomes over a null model by 1.2 percent ([Carroll and Kenkel](https://www.sas.rochester.edu/psc/polmeth/papers/Kenkel_Carroll.pdf)).
- Lind shows the index "codes the Soviet Union as overtaking U.S. power in the 1970s" and "ranks India in 2007 as the world's third-largest power" ([Lind, *International Security* 49(2)](https://direct.mit.edu/isec/article/49/2/7/125214/Back-to-Bipolarity-How-China-s-Rise-Transformed)).
- Beckley counts at least sixty-nine power-measurement frameworks from 1936 to 2010, forty-two of which used gross indicators alone ([Beckley](https://direct.mit.edu/isec/article-abstract/43/2/7/12211)).
- A survey commissioned outside the academy reaches the same conclusion independently: "The notion of a single aggregate quantity of national power existing in the abstract therefore makes little or no sense", and "Aggregated measures of power select and weight variables in an essentially arbitrary way" ([Carnegie Endowment](https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2026/04/methods-of-national-power-analysis-pitfalls-and-best-practices)).

A field that has documented these properties and continued the practice for a thousand studies does not have an information problem. It has an alternatives problem: the scalar answers a question -- who is stronger, by how much -- that nothing else on offer answers at all. Any proposal that removes the scalar owes the field an account of what happens to that question. Section 9 returns to it as an open question rather than pretending it has been dissolved.

## 2.3 Two concessions, made before the argument starts

**First: scalar reduction has repeatedly been found to lose little.** Kugler and Organski operationalise power as economic productivity per capita times population, proxy it with GNP, and report that this "parsimonious and robust measure performed as well as the more complex index of power developed by Singer et al. (1972)" ([Kugler and Organski, pp. 190--191](http://slantchev.ucsd.edu/courses/pdf/Kugler%20&%20Organski%20-%20The%20Power%20Transition.pdf)). Beckley's two-variable product -- GDP times GDP per capita -- predicts 78 percent of wars and 70 percent of militarised disputes over 1816--2010, against CINC's 70 and 64 ([Beckley](https://scispace.com/pdf/the-power-of-nations-measuring-what-matters-441eqyutp6.pdf)). Adding components did not help; multiplying two did. Any argument for a four-element vector must survive the observation that the best predictive record in this literature belongs to a two-variable scalar.

**Second: the arbitrary-weights critique is weaker than it is usually stated.** The Lowy Institute publishes its weights -- Economic Capability 17.5 percent, Military Capability 17.5, Resilience 10, Future Resources 10, Economic Relationships 15, and Defence Networks, Diplomatic Influence and Cultural Influence 10 each -- concedes that "it is of course possible to reach other value judgements about the relative importance of the measures", and then reports that its own "Sensitivity analysis has determined that the large number of indicators ... are quantitatively more important than our weighting scheme" ([Lowy methodology](https://power.lowyinstitute.org/methodology/)). That is a publisher volunteering evidence against the standard critique of its own product. A paper built on the arbitrary-weights objection would be built on the weakest available ground, and this one is not.

## 2.4 The narrower case this paper makes instead

If the vector cannot claim predictive superiority (concession one) and cannot lean on weight arbitrariness (concession two), the case has to be narrower, and it is: **the aggregation step destroys the specific information this research programme is about.** The programme's question is not "who is stronger" but "which domain is moving in which direction, and does the pattern of domain-wise movement match a specific structural hypothesis". Over 2015--2025 the sign of relative US--PRC movement is dimension-dependent within the same years; a scalar over those dimensions reports the sign of its weights, and a reader cannot recover the domain pattern from any scalar at any weighting. The formal statement -- that in additive aggregation, weights are substitution rates, not importances, so the aggregation asserts trade-offs the analyst never argued for -- is section 3's burden ([Munda and Nardo](https://publications.jrc.ec.europa.eu/repository/bitstream/JRC32434/EUR%2021834%20EN.pdf); [OECD/JRC Handbook](https://knowledge4policy.ec.europa.eu/sites/default/files/jrc47008_handbook_final.pdf)).

The rest of the paper is the discipline required to make that narrow case honestly: the measurement model and its precedents (section 4), pre-registration for future-dated conditions with the objections at full strength (section 5), identification stated per quantity (section 6), prior-predictive checking reported including its failures (section 7), what the paper cannot establish (section 8), and the questions it poses without answering (section 9).
