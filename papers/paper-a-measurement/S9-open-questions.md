# Paper A, section 9 -- Open questions the paper poses without answering

**Status.** Draft, 2026-08-20. Posed, not answered; where a partial answer exists in the literature, it is cited as partial.

---

**1. Should categorical falsification conditions be restated as probability forecasts under a strictly proper scoring rule?** Propriety is what makes a quoted probability honest ([Gneiting and Raftery](https://sites.stat.washington.edu/raftery/Research/PDF/Gneiting2007jasa.pdf)), and threshold verdicts discard calibration information a Brier or log score would use. But the conditions were pre-registered as categorical, and restating them now would change registered objects after the fact -- the one move the whole apparatus exists to prevent. The available compromise, registering probability restatements prospectively for future evaluation years while the categorical originals continue to govern, is itself a design question the programme has not committed on.

**2. Is there a non-compensatory aggregation that answers the practitioner's ranking question without smuggling in substitution rates?** The formal objection to additive composites (section 3) does not touch aggregations that refuse trade-offs -- lexicographic orderings, outranking methods. The OECD/JRC Handbook's own constraint gestures at the difficulty: no indicator or dimension may exceed 50 percent of total weight, on pain of becoming "a dictator in Arrow's terminology" ([OECD/JRC Handbook, p. 111](https://knowledge4policy.ec.europa.eu/sites/default/files/jrc47008_handbook_final.pdf)) -- the aggregation literature bumping into social-choice impossibility results suggests the answer is partial at best. If a satisfactory non-compensatory scheme exists, C1's refusal to rank was a choice, not a necessity, and the paper would owe the field a revision.

**3. What is the correct naive benchmark for a fifty-year capability trajectory?** Section 5.6 adopts the mandatory-benchmark consequence, but the benchmark that defeated expert forecasters was a random walk defined over months ([Forecasting Collaborative](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10192018/)). Over fifty years, a random walk in log relative capability is not obviously naive -- it may be close to the least-structured admissible model -- and a benchmark that is itself a serious competitor is no longer a floor. The programme's own answer is registered rather than argued here: a driftless per-element random walk, with innovation scales derived by a pre-committed procedure and applied prospectively only (rule R012 in the transformation-rules registry). The question stays open because that registration settles the programme's practice, not the principle: whoever derives the *correct* naive benchmark for a fifty-year horizon solves it for every long-horizon forecasting exercise, not only this one.

**4. Does the vector's refusal to rank make it unusable for the field that asked the question -- and if so, is that a defect of the vector or of the question?** The scalar survives a thousand studies of documented inadequacy because it answers the question the field asks (section 2.2). The vector answers a different question: which domain is moving in which direction. If the field's question is well posed, the vector is a partial instrument and this paper's contribution is correspondingly partial. If the field's question is not well posed -- if "who is stronger" without a domain and a horizon has no answer capable of being wrong -- then the instrument's refusal is the finding. The paper takes no position; it notes only that both readings are consistent with everything reported here, and that the choice between them is a claim about the field, not about the measurement.
